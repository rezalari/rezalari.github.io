A kind no

You can care about people and still hold a hard standard. The trick is what you ask before you agree.

Reza Larimi 21 September 2026 3 minute read

Kindness can include a firm no.

Most of the leaders I admire are warm. Most of them are also hard to get a yes out of. For a long time I read that as a contradiction. It is not. It is the same trait seen from two sides.

I was reading a Harvard Business Review piece by Lisa Earle McLeod and Elizabeth Lotardo on purpose-driven leadership. Their argument, roughly: leaders who anchor on a shared positive impact burn out less, because purpose makes the hard calls easier. What they call noble-purpose leadership shows up in how you talk to people, how you decide, and how you coach.

The line that stuck with me was not in the article. It came out of it. If purpose is real, it has to be able to say no.

The four questions

Here is what I now ask before the next yes. It takes about a minute.

Purpose. What useful outcome does this create? If nobody can name it in one sentence, that is the answer.

Capacity. What will it actually take? Hours, people, attention, money. Say the number out loud.

Ownership. Who can act and decide? If the answer is “we,” nobody owns it.

Trade-off. What changes if we accept? Every yes moves something else. Name what.

What it sounds like

Not “no.” More like:

“That could be valuable. Which existing priority should we replace?”

That sentence does three things. It takes the idea seriously. It makes the cost visible. And it hands the decision back to the person who brought it, which is where it belongs.

The qualification

A meaningful mission is not a substitute for resources. I have watched purpose get used as a reason to under-staff and over-promise. That is not noble, it is just cheap. Purpose should help us choose. It should never be the excuse for not choosing.

Why this matters in deep tech

Hardware does not care about your intentions. A wrong yes on a build costs months, not a sprint. The four questions are cheap insurance against the expensive kind of enthusiasm.

What helps you hold a clear boundary and keep the relationship? I would like to hear it.

Reading that prompted this: How to Be a Purpose-Driven Leader Without Burning Out, Harvard Business Review, July 2023.
